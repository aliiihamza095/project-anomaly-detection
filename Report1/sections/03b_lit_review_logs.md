**III. LITERATURE REVIEW**

**B. Behavior-based anomaly detection of user logins**

Landauer et al. [1] present UEBA as a way of catching intrusions through compromised accounts by checking present behavior against profiles drawn from past logs [1, Abstract]. Their contribution is a real log dataset from a cloud storage service, covering over 5,000 users and more than five years [1, Abstract]. The data also show why per-user profiles are hard to build in practice: 3,907 of 5,389 users appeared on a single day only, and long-time users kept trying event types they had not used before [1, Secs. IV-A and IV-B], which led the authors to rule out a fixed training period [1, Sec. IV-B]. For testing, they staged an account takeover by exchanging the identifiers of two similar users [1, Sec. V-A]. Their detector, which compares daily event-count vectors with earlier days, reached a 65% true positive rate at a 4.6% false positive rate [1, Sec. V-D].

Benova and Hudec [6] worked at a very different scale: web server logs of software-update requests, with over 8.48 million users in a single day and no labels [6, pp. 6–7]. An Isolation Forest on request counts per 30-minute interval flagged roughly 60,000 anomalies in one model and roughly 18,000 anomalous sequences in another [6, p. 8]. They grouped the most anomalous sequences with DBSCAN and asked security experts to interpret the groups; one expert saw a user’s nearly constant request percentages as a sign of repeated attempts with invalid credentials [6, pp. 10, 17–18]. Without labels, the study could not report precision or recall, and the authors name false positives and negatives, time zones and the absence of real-time analysis as limitations [6, pp. 6, 21].

**C. Anomaly detection methods**

Komadina et al. [3] give the most direct evidence for choosing Isolation Forest, and also a warning about it. In a test of 13 unsupervised models on firewall logs with injected attacks, it came out best without aggregation (AUC PR of 0.11 and F1-score of 0.15) [3, pp. 16–17], yet the authors judged that no unsupervised model was fit for real use, given the low scores and many false positives [3, pp. 17, 23]. Supervised models did far better, for example Kernel Naive Bayes with an F1-score of 0.965 at one aggregation level [3, pp. 21–22]. Benova and Hudec [6, p. 7] had a practical reason for preferring Isolation Forest to DBSCAN: DBSCAN could only cluster subsets of 100,000 users within 64 GB of memory.

**D. Synthetic data for evaluating detectors**

Evaluating a detector requires labeled attacks, and the sources agree that obtaining them is the hard part. Komadina et al. [3] list the usual routes (a controlled testbed, merging captured attack traffic, or replaying public datasets) and point to their cost, manual effort or outdated attacks [3, pp. 2–3]. Their alternative builds attack logs from domain knowledge and the configuration of the security control, inserts them into the organization’s own logs [3, pp. 8–10] and marks them with a label column [3, p. 11]. One injected case is a remote-desktop session with several failed attempts and then a success [3, pp. 10–11]. They also argue that a careful attacker would copy familiar behavior, whereas a first-time connection or one outside working hours would stand out [3, p. 11]. The method assumes well-documented policies and a stable network, and the authors say the quality of the generated logs still needs validation [3, pp. 22–23].
